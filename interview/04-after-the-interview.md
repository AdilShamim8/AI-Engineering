# After the Interview

> The interview is just the beginning (Updated September 16, 2026). What you do after — evaluating offers, negotiating, and starting strong — shapes your career trajectory more than any single interview.

---

## Handling Offers

### Evaluation Criteria

When you receive an offer, evaluate it across these dimensions:

| Criterion | Questions to Ask | Weight |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| **Compensation** | Does it meet your minimum? Is the equity structure fair? | High |
| **Team and manager** | Who will you work with? What's the manager's style? | High |
| **Technical challenge** | Will you work on interesting AI problems? Will you grow? | High |
| **Impact** | Will your work matter to the business? Can you measure it? | Medium |
| **Growth trajectory** | Is there a clear path to the next level? | Medium |
| **Culture** | Do you align with the company's values and working style? | Medium |
| **Mission** | Do you believe in what the company is building? | Medium |
| **Stability** | Is the company financially healthy? What's the runway? | Low–Medium |
| **Location and flexibility** | Remote? Hybrid? Relocation required? | Variable |

### Startup vs. Big Tech Decision Matrix

| Factor | Choose Startup If | Choose Big Tech If |
|--------|------------------|-------------------|
| Risk tolerance | You're comfortable with uncertainty | You value stability |
| Equity potential | You believe in 10x+ equity upside | You prefer known equity value |
| Learning speed | You want to learn everything fast | You want to learn specific domains deeply |
| Ownership | You want end-to-end ownership | You want to specialize |
| Brand value | The startup's brand in AI is strong | You value the big tech brand on your resume |
| AI focus | AI is the core product | AI is one of many product areas |
| Mentorship | You're comfortable being the expert | You want senior mentors in AI |

## Dealing with Rejections

### Common Rejection Patterns

| Pattern | What It Might Mean | What to Learn |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|
| Rejected after recruiter screen | Resume doesn't match the role | Tailor resume more specifically |
| Rejected after technical round | Technical gaps in specific areas | Identify and strengthen weak areas |
| Rejected after system design | Insufficient depth or poor structure | Practice structured system design |
| Rejected after behavioral | Culture misalignment or poor story quality | Prepare better behavioral stories |
| Rejected after take-home | Submission didn't meet evaluation criteria | Review what top submissions look like |
| Ghosted after interviews | Company disorganization or internal changes | Move on — this says more about them |
| Rejected despite good performance | Stronger candidate, team fit, or headcount change | Don't take it personally; try again in 6 months |

### What to Do After Rejection

1. **Ask for feedback.** "I'd appreciate any feedback on areas I could improve." Most companies won't give detailed feedback, but some will.
2. **Debrief honestly.** Where did you struggle? What caught you off guard?
3. **Update your preparation.** Incorporate lessons learned into your prep plan.
4. **Don't over-correct.** One rejection doesn't mean your entire approach is wrong. Look for patterns across multiple rejections before making major changes.
5. **Stay in touch.** If you liked the company, connect with interviewers on LinkedIn and re-apply in 6–12 months.

## Salary Negotiation Strategies for AI Engineers

### The Current Market (2026)

- AI engineering compensation remains above traditional SWE due to demand
- AI-native companies (OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks) pay at or above FAANG levels
- Startups compete on equity upside, not base salary
- Remote roles have seen base salary normalization, but total comp still varies by location

### Negotiation Framework

1. **Know your numbers.** Research compensation ranges before you ever discuss salary.
2. **Never share your current comp first.** If asked, say "I'd prefer to understand the role and range first."
3. **Let them anchor.** Let the company make the first offer.
4. **Counter with data.** "Based on my research and [specific data points], I was expecting [range]."
5. **Negotiate the full package.** Base, equity, signing bonus, level, title, start date, remote work.
6. **Use competing offers.** The most effective lever. "I have another offer at [X], and I'd prefer to join your team. Can we get closer to that range?"
7. **Be professional and enthusiastic.** "I'm really excited about this role and I want to make the numbers work."
8. **Get it in writing.** Verbal offers are not binding. Always get the final offer in writing.

## Equity Evaluation for AI Startups

### Key Questions to Ask

| Question | Why It Matters |
|----------|---------------|
| What's the current valuation? | Determines the value of your equity |
| What's the strike price for options? | Your cost to exercise |
| What's the vesting schedule? | Standard is 4 years with 1-year cliff |
| What's the 409A valuation? | Tax implications of your options |
| What happens in a down round? | Dilution and repricing risk |
| What's the exit timeline? | When (if ever) can you liquidate? |
| Is there a secondary market? | Can you sell shares before IPO/acquisition? |
| What's the preferred stack? | Preferred shareholders get paid first |
| What are the exercise windows? | How long after leaving to exercise options? |
| Has there been a tender offer? | Liquidity events signal company health |

### Equity Red Flags

- **No clear valuation** — if they won't tell you the valuation, that's a problem
- **Very long vesting with no cliff** — non-standard and potentially exploitative
- **No exercise window after leaving** — you lose unexercised options immediately
- **Double-trigger acceleration is missing** — if the company is acquired, your unvested equity should vest
- **Founder selling secondary while asking you to accept below-market comp** — misaligned incentives

## Team Matching Considerations

At large companies (Google, Meta, Amazon), you may need to go through team matching after passing the interview loop. Key factors:

1. **Manager quality** — This is the single biggest factor in your job satisfaction. Ask to meet the manager before accepting.
2. **Team seniority** — Teams with more senior engineers provide better mentorship but less individual ownership.
3. **Product vs. platform** — Product teams build user-facing AI features; platform teams build AI infrastructure. Know which you prefer.
4. **On-call expectations** — AI production systems often require on-call. Understand the rotation and expectations.
5. **Growth opportunities** — Will you be the most senior AI engineer on the team, or will you have room to learn?
6. **Technical stack** — Does the team use tools and approaches you want to learn?

## Reference Checks

Reference checks have become standard at top AI companies in 2026. Be prepared:

### Who to Use as References
- **Direct manager** (most important) — can speak to your impact and growth
- **Senior colleague** — can speak to your technical ability
- **Cross-functional partner** (PM, designer, data scientist) — can speak to your collaboration
- **Direct report** (if you're senior+) — can speak to your leadership

### How to Prepare Your References
1. **Ask permission first.** Always confirm before sharing someone as a reference.
2. **Brief them on the role.** Share the job description and why you're excited about it.
3. **Remind them of your work together.** Send specific projects and accomplishments.
4. **Prepare them for the call.** "They'll probably ask about [X]. Here's what I'd emphasize..."

### What Companies Ask References
- "How does [candidate] handle ambiguous problems?"
- "Can you describe a time [candidate] made a mistake and how they handled it?"
- "How does [candidate] communicate with non-technical stakeholders?"
- "Would you work with [candidate] again?" (This is the most important question)

## Starting Strong in Your First 90 Days

### Week 1–2: Learn
- Understand the AI systems already in production
- Meet your team and key stakeholders
- Read the codebase, documentation, and runbooks
- Set up your development environment
- Understand the model serving and deployment infrastructure

### Week 3–4: Contribute
- Pick up a small, well-scoped task
- Ship your first change to production (even a small one)
- Start building relationships with cross-functional partners
- Understand the evaluation and monitoring infrastructure

### Week 5–8: Build
- Take on a medium-sized feature or improvement
- Start contributing to technical discussions and design reviews
- Identify one area where you can add unique value (evaluation, cost optimization, safety)
- Build trust by delivering on commitments

### Week 9–12: Lead
- Propose an improvement based on your observations
- Start mentoring or onboarding newer team members
- Establish your area of ownership
- Set goals with your manager for the next quarter

### First 90 Days Checklist
- [ ] Shipped at least one meaningful change to production
- [ ] Understood the full AI pipeline (data → training → serving → evaluation)
- [ ] Built relationships with PM, data engineering, and design counterparts
- [ ] Audited production LLM metrics: prompt caching hit-rates, p99 TTFT, and guardrail false positives
- [ ] Identified at least one area for improvement and proposed a solution
- [ ] Established a 1:1 rhythm with your manager
- [ ] Documented something that was previously undocumented
- [ ] Participated in an on-call rotation (if applicable)
- [ ] Set 6-month goals with your manager
