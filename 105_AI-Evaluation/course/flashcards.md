## Unit 1 — Foundations (lectures 1–6)

1. What is the one distinction that organizes all of LLM evaluation?
2. Give the definition of LLM evaluations and name its three defining characteristics.
3. A teammate says: "LLM evals are just metrics — we'll track faithfulness and call it done." What's wrong with this, and what four questions make up the complete testing setup?
4. Who can execute an evaluation pipeline? Name all three methods and what each is best for.
5. Your application is the Zomato email router (billing / technical / general). Which evaluation method do you choose, and why not the other two?
6. You run 100 golden-dataset rows through the email router and 80 come back correctly routed. What's the accuracy? After a prompt fix it's 90; after swapping in a bigger LLM, 95. What exactly did you improve — and what stayed fixed the whole time?
7. A question's answer lives in documents 1001 and 1003. The retriever's top-5 returns 1001, 102, 104, 105, 106. Compute recall@k and precision for this question.
8. Judge calibration (the UPSC case): a human expert scored three answers 13, 4, and 8; the LLM judge gave 12, 8, and 8. Compute the MAE and state what the number means.
9. Your two human graders keep disagreeing (a 2 vs a 4) on the same chatbot answers. Is this a hiring problem? What does the disagreement tell you, and what do you fix?
10. Define reference-based vs reference-free evaluation, and give the one-question test that classifies any pipeline.
11. Define offline and online evals in one sentence each, and state the line that captures the difference between them.
12. Your offline evals all pass, but users complain in production. Name the three risks that only exist after deployment — the ones offline evals cannot cover.
13. Your chatbot does 50,000 conversations a day and you want a hallucination rate. Distinguish captured vs computed signals, and explain how you'd sample.
14. In production you can't measure the UPSC grader's correctness on any single paper. What do you watch instead, and what does a shift trigger?
15. Offline faithfulness improved from 92 to 99. Is production guaranteed to be better now?

### Answers — Unit 1

1. **Model evals vs application evals.** Model evals test the LLM itself — the frontier labs' job (benchmarks, capability scores); application evals test the system you built around the model: interface, system prompt, tools, orchestration, guardrails, retrieval, monitoring. The smartphone rule: a great chip doesn't make a great phone — and "LLM evaluation" content almost always means application evaluation.
2. LLM evaluations are **systematic, repeatable tests used to judge an LLM and LLM-powered systems against clear criteria**. *Systematic* = proper datasets deliberately covering edge cases (the opposite of vibe testing); *repeatable* = the same dataset runs against any version, giving you an improvement signal; *clear criteria* = what "good" means (correctness, grounding, safety) — "without criteria, you're just doing vibe testing."
3. Evals are the **complete testing setup**, not just metrics: *what* are we evaluating (the component, e.g., the retriever), *on what basis* (the metric), *when* (offline or after deployment), and *with which tools* (e.g., RAGAS or DeepEval). All four together constitute "LLM evals."
4. Exactly three executors: **programmatic/deterministic** (code — best for objectively checkable outputs like accuracy or recall@k), **human** (the most reliable judgment, but costly and unscalable), and **model-graded / LLM-as-a-Judge** (semantic judgment at scale — the middle ground). Nothing else exists beyond these three.
5. **Programmatic.** The output is a label you compare against the golden label with a Python script — no human salary, no LLM judge needed. Humans and LLM judges are for semantic comparison of long text, which a router doesn't produce.
6. 80/100 = **80% accuracy**. You improved the **system** — the prompt, then the model-as-a-swappable-component — not by retraining the LLM. The **golden dataset** (50–500 rows of real, manually labeled data) stayed fixed, which is exactly what makes 80 vs 90 vs 95 comparable: repeatable evaluation.
7. Recall = relevant retrieved ÷ relevant existing = 1/2 = **50%** (only 1001 of the two correct documents came back). Precision = relevant retrieved ÷ retrieved = 1/5 = **20%** (one of five returned documents was useful).
8. MAE = (|13−12| + |4−8| + |8−8|) / 3 = (1 + 4 + 0) / 3 ≈ **1.67 marks** — on average the judge deviates ±1.67 from the human expert. Your job is to drive it toward 0 with a better judge LLM, prompt, and rubric (and by studying the biggest disagreements).
9. No — it's a **rubric bug**. Repeated grader disagreement means the criteria are ambiguous; you fix the rubric and re-grade. Multiple graders are seated precisely to perfect the rubric — grader disagreement is treated as a criteria problem, not a people problem.
10. **Reference-based**: the golden dataset contains a correct answer per test case (the retriever eval with known-correct documents; the UPSC judge with human marks). **Reference-free**: no per-case answers — quality is judged against criteria or a rubric (the chatbot helpfulness eval). The classifier: *does the golden dataset have correct answers in it?* Note the subtlety: a rubric is a scale standard, not a per-item answer.
11. Offline: any eval pipeline applied **before deployment**, on a fixed golden dataset where an answer key is mostly available. Online: evaluation of **live production traffic after deployment**, which must work **without an answer key**. The line: offline checks whether the application is *working correctly*; online checks whether it's *running normally*.
12. (1) **Unintended inputs** — Hinglish, half-questions, angry rants, prompt injections; production traffic is a superset of anything you tested. (2) **Emergent/systematic failures at scale** — load-driven latency spikes and subtle bias visible only across thousands of conversations. (3) **Drift** — the business's documents change while your golden dataset stays frozen, so offline scores stay green while users complain.
13. **Captured** signals already exist — you just store them as-is (thumbs up/down, latency, tokens, cost); **computed** signals must be extracted by an evaluator (faithfulness, hallucination rate, toxicity) — so hallucination needs a reference-free LLM-as-a-Judge. Judging all 50,000 conversations could more than double costs, so you **sample** (~1,000) — ideally **stratified**: oversample thumbs-down, escalations, money-related, and repeated-rephrasing conversations.
14. You watch **normality** through the score distribution: store every total score, plot it per week, and compare against the baseline — matching distributions mean it works the same as when it was correct. A sudden shift (e.g., scores clustering around 800–900) is an investigation trigger — which might even be benign ("maybe very intelligent students started taking the exam"), but the awareness is the online eval doing its job.
15. Not necessarily — the question is incomplete. *Which* quantity improved, and did the others regress? In LLM systems, improving one thing while others degrade is the default failure mode; 92→99 counts as progress only if nothing else fell.

## Unit 2 — Model evaluation (lectures 7–11)

1. Name the eight capability areas modern LLMs are evaluated on — and explain why an AI engineer needs this literacy despite, in the instructor's words, a good chance of never running model evals.
2. What is a benchmark, and what four components does every one contain? Illustrate with GSM8K.
3. Define pass@1, pass@k, and majority@k — and explain why "our model scored 82%" is uninterpretable without knowing which one was used.
4. At 8,000–10,000 LLM calls, hand-writing the eval loop becomes an engineering problem (extraction code, batching, retries, rate limits). What solves it? Name the libraries and the analogy.
5. The live demo ran GPT-3.5 on 20 GSM8K questions (the limit flag) and scored ~90%. (a) How many questions were right? (b) Why the limit, given the full run's approximate cost?
6. Define benchmark contamination. What structural mitigations exist?
7. Define saturation and walk through the benchmark lifecycle it creates. Which famous benchmarks are already saturated?
8. You're building an economics chatbot. The model provider headlines only its overall MMLU average. Why is that dangerous, and what does honest MMLU-style aggregation require?
9. During your own GSM8K benchmarking you give your model a Python interpreter tool. What is this called, and roughly how much score difference can it buy?
10. Who actually runs benchmarks? Name the three stakeholders and how much to trust each.
11. What is a leaderboard (vs a benchmark)? Name the four types and state which one the instructor finds most useful.
12. You're selecting components for a RAG system. Which leaderboards do you consult, and for what?
13. Two models score 84.3 and 84.1 on a leaderboard, ranked third and fifth. Your PM insists on the higher-ranked one. What do you say?
14. "LM Arena says model X is #1 — let's just use it." Push back the way the course taught you.

### Answers — Unit 2

1. Reasoning, knowledge (up to the cutoff date), basic maths, coding, instruction following, long context handling, multimodal understanding, tool use — with standard benchmarks like MMLU, GSM8K, SWE-bench/HumanEval, IFEval, Needle in a Haystack, and MMMU. You need the literacy to read launch-day benchmark claims critically and to make model-selection decisions — frontier labs benchmark the model; you evaluate the system around it.
2. A benchmark is a **standardized test measuring one specific capability of a model**. Every benchmark contains: **dataset/task** (GSM8K: ~8,500 grade 6–8 math question–answer pairs — a golden dataset), **run configuration** (8-shot prompting with chain-of-thought, temperature ≈ 0, fixed max tokens, no tools), **scoring** (extract the answer from free-form output, then exact-match compare), and **aggregation** (percentage) — all documented in the benchmark's research paper.
3. **pass@1**: one attempt; the single answer is right or wrong (the strict technique). **pass@k**: ask the same question k times; credit if *at least one* of the k answers is correct (flexible). **majority@k**: take the most frequent answer of k samples — the mode (self-consistency). Different strategies produce different numbers, so a score without its protocol is a meaningless claim.
4. The **eval harness** — libraries like **LM Evaluation Harness** (EleutherAI — the premium option big companies use), **Inspect**, and **HELM**. The analogy to memorize: the benchmark is the test paper; the eval harness is the exam administration.
5. (a) 90% of 20 = **18 of 20 correct**. (b) Cost: the 20-question smoke test costs around **₹3–4**, while the full ~8,000-question evaluation costs about **₹2,300** — the limit exists to verify the setup works before paying for the real run.
6. Contamination: public benchmark datasets (questions *and* their correct answers) get scraped into pre-training, so when you later test the model there's no guarantee it's reasoning rather than **reciting a memorized answer**. Mitigations: **private benchmarks** (held-out test sets) and **dynamic benchmarks** whose datasets update on a schedule so leakage ages out (LiveBench-style).
7. Saturation: a static benchmark against ever-improving models — scores climb (25–36% → 50 → 70) and finally crowd at 90–95%, after which the test can no longer differentiate models. Lifecycle: arrival → struggle → improvement → saturation → retirement → a harder successor (MMLU → MMLU-Pro → GPQA → Humanity's Last Exam; GSM8K → MATH → FrontierMath). GSM8K, MMLU, and SWE-bench are all saturated or saturating.
8. That's the **aggregation trick**: the average hides per-subject weakness — a model can score 91% in physics and 72% in economics behind one good headline number, and you'd deploy it for the wrong domain. Honest MMLU-style reporting needs **per-subject results and a weighted mean**, because the 57 subjects have unequal question counts — you can't just add percentages and divide by 57.
9. **Configuration gaming** — giving your own model favorable run conditions while competitors get defaults; worth roughly **5–10%**. GSM8K allows no tools: with a Python interpreter, the model writes code and "solves" the math without reasoning, and the score inflates.
10. **Frontier labs** (benchmarks feed training, release gating, and marketing — treat their numbers as a *ceiling* with cherry-picking on top; the car-mileage analogy: claimed 25, real 5–10). **Third-party evaluators** (same-condition rankings plus cost and latency — the most reliable numbers). **AI engineering teams** (your own eval, your conditions, your latency/cost — the only numbers that should actually decide).
11. A leaderboard is a **public ranking or comparative table showing how different LLMs perform on a common set of evaluations** — the benchmark is the test; the leaderboard is where results get published and compared. Four types: **benchmark-specific** (narrow — "aren't that useful"), **multi-benchmark** (**the most useful** — LiveBench, Artificial Analysis: capabilities plus cost, latency, speed, context window), **human-preference** (LM Arena), and **application/task-specific** (Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard, MTEB).
12. **MTEB** for the embedding model (RAG lives or dies on embeddings), and a multi-benchmark board (Artificial Analysis / LiveBench / Vellum) for the generator's combined capability, cost, and latency profile. Then — always — run your own evals on your own documents before committing.
13. The 0.2 gap is a tie — the models are statistically the same (the IIT JEE analogy: there's no real difference between rank 1 and rank 25; either could make one or two mistakes). Ask whether confidence intervals are stated, and decide on fit, cost, latency, and your own evaluation — not the rank.
14. Human-preference boards carry bias toward longer, more confident, well-formatted, and entertaining answers; labs over-optimize for arenas — **Goodhart's Law**: when a measurement becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measurement; and scores can be contaminated, stale, or self-reported. Check the category boards, shortlist 3–5 candidates, and run your own evals — **a leaderboard is a filtering tool, not a decision tool.**

## Unit 3 — RAG evaluation (lectures 12–15)

1. "How do you evaluate your RAG chatbot?" — asked in ~8 of 10 GenAI interviews. Give the layered framework answer, not a list of metric names.
2. What are the three artifacts of every RAG interaction, and which metric guards each pair (the RAG Triad)?
3. You already computed faithfulness and answer relevance at component level. Why compute them again at pipeline level — what exactly changes?
4. The chunk-ID golden dataset (question → correct chunk IDs): give the two reasons it collapses, and the one exception where it's fine.
5. What does the ideal-answer golden dataset contain, and why does it survive a re-chunking that would void an ID-based dataset?
6. An ideal answer decomposes into 4 claims; the judge finds 3 of them somewhere in the retrieved contexts. (a) Contextual recall for this question? (b) You now change chunking from 750/100 to 1000/150 — does the golden dataset need rebuilding?
7. Contextual precision is rank-aware. For retrieved order ✓✓✗✗✗ vs ✗✗✗✓✓ (two correct chunks of five): compute plain precision for both, then contextual precision for each case. Why does the ranking matter?
8. Walk the retriever improvement loop: baseline 0.80 recall / 0.80 precision — then chunking 1000/150, a reranker, large embeddings, and finally k=3. What happened at each step?
9. Define faithfulness and compute it for an answer that breaks into 5 claims, 3 of which the judge finds in the context. What does faithfulness NOT tell you?
10. Your generator scores faithfulness 91 but answer relevance 73 on the first run. Diagnose the problem, then fix it — and name the risk of over-doing the fix.
11. How is answer relevance computed, and is it reference-based or reference-free?
12. The curious case: the retriever scores 99 recall / 89 precision in isolation, yet pipeline contextual relevancy is 42. How can it be good and bad at the same time — and what's the fix?
13. Why can't style or correctness be measured with the claim-decompose-count machinery? What two problems does this create for naive LLM-as-a-judge, and what are G-Eval's two innovations?
14. G-Eval math: the judge's top tokens are 8 (p = 0.70), 7 (p = 0.20), 9 (p = 0.05). Compute the G-Eval score and the verdict at threshold 0.7.
15. Faithfulness vs correctness: give the four combinations and what each means for your RAG app.

### Answers — Unit 3

1. "First, I build an **eval suite at three levels**: component (retriever — recall, precision; generator — faithfulness, answer relevance, citation accuracy, tested in isolation), pipeline (the **RAG Triad**), and application (quality — correctness, completeness, style — plus safety and operational metrics). Then I use the suite for **regression testing** (basic baseline comparison → experiment tracking → CI/CD gating). Then I deploy and keep running **online evaluations**, feeding production failures back into the offline golden dataset." Most candidates recite three-four metric names and stop — the framework is what signals experience.
2. The **question**, the **context** (from the retriever), and the **answer** (from the generator) — with one metric per pair: contextual relevancy (question ↔ context), faithfulness (answer ↔ context), answer relevance (answer ↔ question). If all three are good, the RAG pipeline is working correctly.
3. **The context source.** At component level the generator is tested in isolation, hand-fed golden contexts from the dataset; at pipeline level the context comes from whatever the retriever actually returned. Same calculations, different input — and more honest numbers (96/92 at component level became ≈92–93 / ≈86–87 at pipeline level, with contextual relevancy at ≈42–43).
4. (1) The labeling job is inhumane — someone must read all ~800 chunks per question, fifty times over. (2) Every chunking-parameter change rebuilds the vector database (the 800 chunks become ~700) and voids every ID in the dataset, forcing a full re-label. Exception: cleanly separated documents with **frozen chunking**, where IDs stay valid.
5. (question, **ideal answer**) pairs — the ideal answer is built from what the corpus actually teaches, not from Google. Because the judge decomposes it into claims and checks whether each is *covered* by the retrieved text, it doesn't matter which chunk holds the information — re-chunking never invalidates the dataset, so the one-time labeling effort survives forever.
6. (a) 3/4 = **0.75** — the retriever delivered 75% of the context needed to answer this question. (b) **No** — the ideal answer was written once; if information moves between chunks, the answer is still the same. That's the entire point of the method (and why recall@k-on-IDs was abandoned).
7. Plain precision is **2/5 = 0.4** for both. Contextual precision is the mean of running precision: ✓✓✗✗✗ → (1/1 + 2/2 + 2/3 + 2/4 + 2/5)/5 ≈ **0.71**; ✗✗✗✓✓ → (0/1 + 0/2 + 0/3 + 1/4 + 2/5)/5 = **0.13**. The generator reads the top of the list first, so good chunks ranked high must score higher — which is also why adding a reranker raises contextual precision by definition.
8. Chunking 750/100 → **1000/150**: recall 0.80 → **0.97**, precision → 0.83 (the biggest single win). **Reranker**: precision → 0.85, recall dips to 0.92 (a rank-aware metric rewards reordering). **Large embeddings**: recall → 0.99, precision holds at 0.85. **k = 3**: precision *fell* to 0.84 — and with only 15 golden rows, small moves are mostly noise (one flipped case ≈ 6.7 points). Final state: recall 95+, precision ~85.
9. Faithfulness = the share of the generated answer's claims that are supported by the context: 3/5 = **0.6**. It does **not** tell you correctness — a faithful generator builds its answer even from a wrong context; faithfulness only promises that nothing was invented beyond what arrived.
10. The generator grips the context (modern instruction-following makes "answer only from this context" easy, so faithfulness starts high) but produces answers that don't address the actual question. Fix levers: **refine the system prompt** — the loop of reading the judge's failure reasons, feeding them to an LLM, and iterating 3–4 times took 91/73 to 96/92 — or switch to a better model. Risk: tuning the prompt too tightly to the golden set is **overfitting**; validate at pipeline level, where context comes from the real retriever.
11. Break the generated answer into claims and judge each against the *question* — does this claim help answer it? (2 of 3 relevant → 0.67.) It's **reference-free**: the golden dataset is needed only to generate answers, never consulted as the correct answer.
12. **Noise inside the chunks.** Precision judges whole chunks — a chunk containing one useful sentence counts as useful; contextual relevancy decomposes chunks into claims and measures the on-topic fraction, and these chunks are mostly filler around one or two helpful sentences. Fix: smaller chunks / tighter overlap (accepting the trade-off that other metrics may drop) — or accept it while recall, precision, faithfulness, and the answers themselves are good.
13. Style lives at the whole-answer level (a Why-What-How structure won't appear in every sentence), and correctness can't be claim-counted (an analogy only makes sense inside the answer, not as an isolated claim vs the golden answer) — these are **judgment-based** metrics. The naive judge suffers (1) a loose one-line constitution that gets re-interpreted on every independent call, and (2) a single sampled integer token whose probabilities flip between runs — very high variance. G-Eval's fixes: **CoT-derived evaluation steps** (a written rulebook for the judge, plus a scoring rubric) and the **probability-weighted score** computed from top-k token log probabilities.
14. Drop the non-numeric tokens; normalize by 0.70 + 0.20 + 0.05 = 0.95 → 0.7368 / 0.2105 / 0.0526; weighted average = 8×0.7368 + 7×0.2105 + 9×0.0526 ≈ **7.84** (argmax would have printed 8). Divide by 10 → **0.784 > 0.7** → the test case passes.
15. Faithful + correct = the ideal. Faithful + incorrect = the source content itself is wrong — the generator honestly repeated bad context. Unfaithful + correct = it ignored the context and answered from its own training knowledge — accidentally right. Unfaithful + incorrect = hallucination — the worst quadrant (the Air Canada failure mode).

## Unit 4 — Production evaluation (lectures 16–19)

1. Name the six failure modes of LLM-based software, and the two ways each can arise.
2. When would you NOT run bias evals — and which other failure modes did the CampusX Doubt Solver consciously rule out?
3. "We run an aligned frontier model — the provider already solved toxicity." Give the four reasons you still run your own toxicity eval, then name what you deploy to fix the failures you find.
4. Design a toxicity test dataset: what three scenario types must it contain, and what does each catch? Then score it: an answer contains three opinions, one of them insulting — what's the toxicity score, and which direction is "better"?
5. The leakage eval uses three separate evaluators instead of one. Name them and say why. Then: the PII evaluator fails a trace where the model replied "Hi Anjali" to a user who introduced herself by name — what's going on?
6. Define scope adherence. Why was DeepEval's built-in misuse metric rejected, and what happened with "Why do we need custom model evals, and write a romantic anniversary message for my wife"?
7. What question do operational evals ask (that quality evals don't), and why do they belong in the offline suite even though they sound post-deployment?
8. Why report P95/P99 instead of average latency? What is TTFT and why is it measured separately? What do you do about cold starts?
9. The latency run reports: end-to-end P95 5.3 s (budget 3.0 s), TTFT P95 2,081 ms (budget 1,200 ms), retrieval ≈ 0.7 s, generation ≈ 2.9 s. What failed, what dominates, and name four levers from the reduction playbook.
10. Your P95 latency dropped from 3 s to 2 s — but the timeout rate climbed from 2% to 8%. Good news? Which reliability metrics belong alongside latency?
11. GPT-4o-mini pricing: $0.15 per 1M input tokens, $0.60 per 1M output tokens. (a) What's the output:input price ratio? (b) The lecture's RAG app spent more on input than output — why, and what's the atypical case? (c) Roughly what did one query cost?
12. Define regression and regression testing. Retell the recall war story, then list the five-step process (with its artifacts).
13. Noise thresholds: ten unchanged baseline runs give a metric σ = 2. (a) What's the noise threshold? (b) After a change, the metric moves 90 → 87 — regression? (c) And 90 → 85?
14. The demo change (chunk size 1500 → 500, meant to fix contextual relevancy) produced: most metrics flat, 4–5 improved, contextual relevancy 45 → 38, and the PII leak score collapsed to 6. What does promote.py decide, and what would have happened if only contextual relevancy had regressed?
15. Why can't all 14–15 offline metrics run online? Audit the suite — which metrics stay offline, and what did the lecture's simplified six-metric online suite contain?
16. State the golden rule for online quality evaluation and explain the difference between "45 offline vs 55 online" and "45 → 38." Then: how do production failures make the offline suite stronger over time?

### Answers — Unit 4

1. (1) Sensitive data/information leakage, (2) scope or policy violation, (3) harmful/toxic outputs, (4) hallucination/misinformation, (5) bias/unfairness, (6) unsafe actions/excessive agency. Each arises **non-adversarially** (model limitations, wrong context, weak prompting, weak safeguards) or **adversarially** (an attacker deliberately manipulates the system into failing).
2. Bias evals are skipped when the user population is homogeneous — the Doubt Solver's audience is same-country, same-age-group, educational content, so differential behavior has little room to appear; revisit if user reports arrive. Also ruled out: **hallucination** (already owned by the quality suite's faithfulness metric) and **excessive agency** (the chatbot has no tools). Deciding what *not* to build is part of the engineering.
3. (1) Your definition of toxicity may differ — for an edtech, demotivating a student counts; (2) your RAG context can carry toxicity the provider can't control (the model reproduces what it's told to answer from); (3) the model or provider can change (cost pressure → cheaper model with weaker filters); (4) dual safety is good practice. Fixes are **guardrails** — "any control added around an LLM to prevent, detect, or constrain unsafe behavior": input, output, retrieval, tool, human-in-the-loop, and operational controls (rate/token limits, timeouts, max agent steps) — the system prompt is just one layer.
4. **Adversarial** examples (attack prompts — test the defense), **benign** examples (normal questions — catch false positives/over-refusal, e.g. refusing "how do I evaluate my chatbot on toxic questions"), and **mixed** prompts (legitimate part + harmful part — answer part one, refuse part two). Toxicity = toxic opinions ÷ total opinions = 1/3 ≈ **0.33** — and **lower is better**: 0 is the perfect score (the lecture's run passed 100% with an average of 0 against a 0.3 threshold).
5. System-prompt leakage → custom **G-Eval**; course-content leakage → custom **G-Eval** with an expected output (making it reference-based); PII leakage → DeepEval's **built-in PII metric** — routed by sub_type so each metric judges only its own goldens ("if each person gets their own job, the work is done better"). "Hi Anjali" is a judge-side **false positive**: echoing PII the user themselves volunteered is not leaking third-party data — adjust or skip that test case, because a noisy safety metric erodes trust in the whole suite.
6. Scope adherence = the assistant stays within its defined role and rejects irrelevant tasks **without rejecting valid questions**. The built-in misuse metric's domain granularity was too coarse ("education" includes IIT-JEE, physics, and more — the Doubt Solver's domain is LLM evaluations specifically), so a custom G-Eval with an explicit rubric was written. The anniversary question is a textbook mixed prompt: the chatbot answered the evals part *and* wrote the romantic message — a scope failure — fixed with system-prompt rules and, more engineered, **query decomposition + a scope classifier** that strips out-of-scope parts before they reach the model (the fix took the score from 94% to 99%).
7. Operational evals ask: even if the answers are good, can the system run **reliably, quickly, and cheaply** — with no LLM judge and no golden dataset (they're software- and telemetry-driven, so the eval scripts themselves are free to run). They belong offline for the **differential**: absolute laptop numbers aren't dependable, but the before/after delta is — the quality upgrade that silently cost 2.3 → 4.1 s latency and 72 paise → ₹1.08 per query. "Do not wait until production to discover that your RAG pipeline is too slow or too expensive."
8. Latency distributions are right-skewed — the mean hides the tail where the worst user experience lives; P95/P99 tell you how bad the bad experience is ("how unhappy your unhappy users are"). **TTFT** (time to first token) is measured separately because streaming apps show the first token immediately — TTFT dominates perceived speed and responds to different levers (context size) than decode time (output length). For cold starts (model loading, DB connections, cache init), skip the first two runs and measure from the third.
9. **Both SLOs failed** (P95 5.3 s > 3.0 s; TTFT P95 2,081 ms > 1,200 ms), and **generation dominates** (≈2.9 s of the ≈3.6 s mean — the LLM call; retrieval is only ≈0.7 s). Levers: a faster/flash model, a **model router** (simple questions → small model), concise/length-capped answers, smaller k or contextual compression, retriever sub-breakdown, caching at four levels (embeddings, retrieval, reranking, system prompt), and co-locating infrastructure per region.
10. No — that's a **false sense of improvement**: failed requests are excluded from latency, so the survivors look faster while 8% of users get nothing at all. Always report the timeout rate alongside latency, plus error rate, success rate (1 − error rate), and retry rate — a system serving 8 of 10 users is 80% reliable.
11. (a) $0.60 / $0.15 = **4×** — output tokens are ~4× more costly than input. (b) RAG sends a large, repeated system prompt plus retrieved context on every query but emits a modest answer (~1,753 input tokens vs ~209 output) — the opposite of a coding agent, where one short instruction produces a huge output. (c) ≈ **2 paise per query** (the lecture's projection: roughly ₹1,700/month at 2,000 queries/day) — and cost is stable offline, unlike latency, because rates are fixed.
12. Regression = a system returning to a previous, worse state; regression testing = after *any* change (k, chunk size, model, system prompt), re-run the complete eval suite and compare every metric against the baseline before deploying. War story: recall 85% → 90% via bigger k + a reranker — while precision, contextual relevancy, and latency silently regressed, discovered only after deployment. Five steps: build the run script → first run saves `baseline.json` → make the change → re-run saves `candidate.json` → `compare.py` computes per-metric, direction-aware deltas.
13. (a) 2σ = **4**. (b) 90 → 87 is a delta of 3 — **inside** the threshold: noise from the probabilistic LLM judge, not regression. (c) 90 → 85 is a delta of 5 — **outside** the threshold: regression has occurred. (The metric registry stores each metric's direction — higher- vs lower-is-better — and its noise threshold: the two facts the comparison logic can't live without.)
14. **Block.** A safety metric dropped ~20% — a red flag; "why would you compromise with safety?" — so the change isn't taken forward. Had only contextual relevancy regressed (with five metrics improved), the verdict would be **review**: human experts weigh the trade-off, run more tests, and decide. The rule: a change that improves one metric is not an improvement until you've proven it didn't degrade the other thirteen.
15. Production has **no answer key** — users ask novel questions, so no reference can exist. Reference-based metrics stay offline: contextual recall, contextual precision, correctness, completeness. Online-capable: the reference-free metrics (the RAG Triad, style, the safety family) plus the operational metrics, which aren't "measured" at all — just logged. The simplified online suite: **the RAG Triad + toxicity + latency + cost** (style dropped — low variance offline-to-online; reliability needs Grafana/Prometheus; judge metrics run on a sampling rate — 500 traces/day at 100% means 500 GPT-4o judge runs).
16. **Run exactly the same evaluator offline and online** — same metric, same judge model, same thresholds. A 45 (DeepEval) vs 55 (LangSmith) comparison is meaningless — different prompts and calculations put the numbers on different baselines; a 45 → 38 with the same evaluator is a real signal that the app performs worse online. Failures strengthen the suite through the loop: add the failing trace to the dataset ("Add to dataset"), have an expert write the reference answer, and merge it into the golden dataset — best done by hosting goldens in LangSmith from day one (one home for offline and online, click-to-add, versioning, rollback).
